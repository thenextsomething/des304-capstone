Eight generation prompts for the Compass character who shoots stills and motion, merged
into one person. Written 14 September 2026 against `concept-notes/compass-product-definition.md`,
`concept-notes/film-script-v1.md` and `concept-notes/world-building.md`. Every interface
element below is already ruled in those files. Nothing here invents a feature.

## The hard rule about what these are for

**These are concept and ideation material only.** They exist to show people the vision,
to test framing and interface placement before the shoot, and to argue the film on a wall
in front of tutors. They are not the film and they are not a source of finished frames.

The finished film is real footage, shot on a real camera with a real person, with the
Compass interface composited on in After Effects. That has been the plan since the
compositing plan was written into the script, and nothing generated from this file
replaces a single frame of it.

Two things follow from that and both are non-negotiable, repeated again at the bottom of
this file:

1. Every generation gets logged with tool, model, the exact prompt and the date.
2. Nothing generated from this file goes near the A3 posters.

## The four features, and where they came from

The character's interface is not designed from taste. All four elements came out of a real
conversation with Navia, a working photographer, on 28 July 2026, and were confirmed as
real Compass features on 12 September:

1. A shot checklist on the lens, what is taken and what is still owed.
2. A review of the last frame without leaving the camera.
3. Reference frames and moodboards from the brief, including work the client liked.
4. A light reading with a small directional diagram.

Prompts 2, 3, 4 and 7 each carry one of them. That is deliberate, so the set covers the
grounded feature list rather than the pretty half of it.

# House look

Every prompt below inherits this block. It is written out here once so the eight
generations read as one set rather than eight unrelated films. If you change something
here, change it in all eight.

## World rule, above everything else

**The environment stays real, recognisable and completely ordinary. The interface is the
only speculative layer in the frame.** An Auckland garage in 2028 is an Auckland garage
today. No glowing surfaces, no wall panels, no floating anything, no clean white product
world, no robots, no holograms, no drone, no future car. If a viewer can point at anything
other than the lens graphics and say "that is the future", the frame has failed.

## The subject lock

Paste this description into every prompt verbatim so the same person comes back each time:

> a man in his early thirties, close-cropped dark hair, light stubble, a plain charcoal
> merino t-shirt under an unbuttoned faded olive canvas overshirt, sleeves pushed up, dark
> work trousers, worn brown leather boots, a thin black camera strap across his chest

**Swap this to match whoever actually gets cast** before you generate anything you intend
to show. The point of the concept frames is that they resemble the film, and casting is
friends and family, so the real person's description beats this one the moment they say yes.

## The glasses

Plain matte black titanium frame, thin rectangular lenses, light and understated, reads as
ordinary prescription eyewear rather than hardware. 38 grams. No visible camera, no visible
electronics, no coloured accent, no logo, no temple bulge, no wire, no earpiece, no light
on the frame. From the front they are just glasses. Nearest real-world comparison for the
silhouette, useful as a reference image if you feed one in: Even Realities G2.

## Camera and format, all eight

- 16:9, 24fps, shallow but not paper-thin depth of field, full-frame equivalent.
- Digital cinema look, mild filmic grain, clean highlight rolloff, no lens flare added,
  no anamorphic streaks, no vignette.
- Colour: honest and slightly cool in the shadows, warm where the sun actually is.
  Auckland daylight is high-UV and clean rather than hazy-golden. Not a teal and orange grade.
- **One camera movement per prompt, never two.** These models degrade fast when you stack
  moves. If a prompt says locked off, it means locked off.
- Third-person shots run 5 to 8 seconds. Point-of-view shots run 4 to 6.

## The interface, exactly

Monochrome binocular waveguide. Warm white, around #FAF4EA, at roughly 70 per cent opacity.
**One type weight throughout**, a light geometric sans, all lowercase or sentence case, never
bold, never condensed. **No borders, no boxes, no brackets, no corner ticks, no crosshairs,
no progress rings, no connecting lines, no drop shadows.** Elements **fade** in and out over
about a third of a second. Nothing ever slides, flips, pops, snaps or switches.

Positions, fixed across the whole set:

| Element | Position | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Last-frame review | Top left, small 16:9 rectangle about one eighth of frame width | Fades up the instant the shutter fires, holds about a second, fades out |
| Shot checklist | Top right, four or five short lines, small tick marks against some | Fades up when the camera comes down, holds, fades |
| Reference and moodboard strip | Along the bottom edge, three frames in a row | **Full colour**, thin warm-white hairline around each frame |
| Light reading | top right, underneath the shot checklist, two small numerals and a plain circle with one arrow in it | Fades up while he reads the light, fades when he lifts the camera |

Two rules that override everything above:

- **The centre of vision is always empty.** The middle 60 per cent of the frame carries no
  graphic at any point, in any prompt, ever. This is the product's entire argument.
- **Graphics sit just inside the frame edges, not flush to them,** so the display reads as
  having an edge of its own rather than being painted over the whole picture.

The reference layer is the one exception to monochrome, and it is an exception on purpose:
a reference is no use to a photographer in the wrong colours, so that layer is full colour
with a thin hairline frame while everything else is warm white and borderless.

## New Zealand, specifically

- **Auckland, and it has to look like Auckland.** Weatherboard villas, corrugated iron,
  roller doors, damp tarmac, volcanic cones on the skyline, pōhutukawa, nīkau, fast-moving
  cumulus off the Tasman, light that changes every thirty seconds.
- **The sun sits in the northern sky.** Midday light comes from the north and shadows fall
  south, which is the opposite of every American reference the models have been trained on.
  Say the direction in plain terms in the prompt so it does not default.
- No American street furniture: no yellow fire hydrants, no left-hand-drive cars, no US
  power poles, no chain-link and stop signs, no clapboard suburbia, no palm-lined boulevard.
- Cars are right-hand drive on black-on-white plates, and mostly a bit older than you expect.
- Set dressing for a person doing small paid jobs: a paper shot list on a clipboard, gaffer
  tape on a secondhand light stand, a folded reflector leaning on a wall, a chilly bin used
  as a seat, a chest freezer, a washing machine, an old bike, a half-empty shelf of paint tins.
  Modest and real. This person is not running a commercial set.

## Shared negative prompt

Veo and Kling take this in a separate negative field. Seedance reads it inline, so append it
to the end of the prompt with "Avoid:" in front. It is repeated at the end of each prompt
block below so each one is genuinely paste-ready.

> holographic HUD, heads-up display, sci-fi interface, video game overlay, targeting
> reticle, crosshair, scanning lines, blue glow, neon, cyan, bounding boxes, floating
> panels, radial menus, data readouts, glowing eyes, visible screens on the glasses,
> camera brand logos, readable body text, paragraphs of text, watermark, subtitles,
> distorted hands, six fingers, extra fingers, merged fingers, warped camera body,
> two cameras, floating camera, beauty retouching, plastic skin, teal and orange grade,
> lens flare, slow motion, whip pan, zoom, crash zoom, American street signage,
> left-hand-drive cars

## Which model to aim at

All of these sit inside Higgsfield. Primary and fallback are named on each prompt.

| Model | What it is best at here |
|---|---|
| **Veo 3.1** | Outdoor and atmospheric realism, light physics, environments. The third-person and location work. |
| **Seedance 2.0** | Prompt adherence and multi-reference input, up to nine references. The point-of-view frames where the interface has to land where you told it to. |
| **Kling 3.0** | Character consistency across cuts and 4K out. Keeping the same face across the whole set, and the hero portrait. |
| **MiniMax Hailuo 2.3** | Holds on-screen text and graphics sharper than the others. The fallback whenever the interface turns to mush. |

**The two-pass method, and use it.** For every point-of-view prompt, generate it twice:
once exactly as written with the interface described, and once with the interface sentences
deleted so you get a clean plate. The clean plate plus a real After Effects overlay will
beat the model's version of your interface every single time, and it is also the honest
rehearsal for how the actual film gets made.

If a model starts ignoring instructions, cut the prompt from the bottom up. The order in
each block is deliberate: camera, subject, action, environment, lighting, interface, mood.
The mood sentence is the cheapest thing to lose.

# Things to avoid

These are the failures that turn up again and again on this exact kind of work. Check every
generation against the list before you keep it.

**Hands on a camera.** The single hardest thing in this whole set. Expect fingers that merge
into the grip, a thumb on the wrong side of the body, a strap that passes through a wrist, or a
seventh finger appearing on the shutter. Mitigations that actually work: keep both hands doing
one simple continuous action rather than a fiddly one, let the camera body cover part of the
hands, shoot the point-of-view frames with the camera entering from the bottom edge so less of
the hand is visible, and generate more takes than you think you need. If a take is otherwise
perfect and the hands are wrong, it is still useful as a framing test. It is not useful as a
shareable frame.

**Text.** None of these models can render five words of English reliably. The checklist will
come back as plausible marks, not language. That is fine and expected for concept work. The
trap is trying to fix it by asking for larger, bolder or more contrasted text, which is exactly
the road to a game HUD. Accept the marks, prove the placement, typeset the real words in After
Effects.

**Game heads-up display.** The default failure mode of every one of these models when you say
the word interface. Watch for: cyan or blue, glow and bloom, bounding boxes around objects,
crosshairs and reticles, corner brackets, scanning lines, radial menus, progress rings, tiny
numbers everywhere, and a graphic parked in the middle of the frame. Compass is warm white, one
type weight, no borders, fades rather than switches, and never occupies the centre. If a
generation has any cyan in it, bin it rather than grading it out. The colour is a symptom of the
model having reached for the wrong reference entirely, and the rest of the frame will be wrong too.

**Camera brand logos.** Models love to hallucinate a red dot, a white lettermark or a brand
badge onto a camera body, and a fake launch film with a real camera brand in it is a legal and
credibility problem at once. Keep the camera matte black and unmarked in the prompt, and check
the pentaprism, the lens barrel ring and the strap, which are the three places it appears.

**Anything that makes the glasses look like they are taking the photograph.** This is the most
damaging failure available, because it inverts the product. Symptoms: a lens or aperture on the
frame, a small light on the temple, the graphics behaving like a camera viewfinder with a shutter
animation, a frame line drawn around the subject, or the wearer not holding a camera at all.
Compass assists the person holding the camera. It never captures, never produces an image and
never shoots anything. If a generation reads as the glasses doing the work, it is not a near
miss, it is the opposite of the film.

**The interface appearing before he acts.** Ordering is the whole argument. The review fades up
after the shutter fires, not before. The checklist appears as the camera comes down, not while
he is shooting. If the model puts anything on the lens during the act of putting the glasses on,
the take is dead regardless of how good it looks.

**Something in the centre of the frame.** Non-negotiable and easy to miss on a quick review,
because a centred graphic often looks composed and deliberate. Check it every time.

**America.** The models default to it hard. Watch for left-hand-drive cars, US road signage,
fire hydrants, clapboard suburbs, palm trees of the wrong kind, and a sun path that puts midday
light in the southern sky. Auckland has its own look and this project has a specific reason to
get it right.

**Over-production.** This character does small paid jobs out of a garage. Two flags and a
seamless white cyclorama, a crew, an assistant or an expensive commercial set all make the film
about a professional studio rather than about an everyday creative, which is the audience the
project has chosen.

**Stacked camera moves.** A push in and a pan in the same prompt gives you neither, plus warping.
One move, or none.

# Two rules that override everything above

## Log every generation

Every clip or frame produced from this file goes in the project's running AI-use log with:

- **Tool:** Higgsfield
- **Model:** the exact model and version used, for example Veo 3.1 or Seedance 2.0
- **Prompt:** the full prompt text as pasted, including the negative prompt
- **Date:** the date generated

The course runs every assignment as Lane 2, so AI use is permitted but must be acknowledged, and
the acknowledgement was ruled to land in the A3 visual project report rather than on the board.
The university's referencing guidance for generative tools is at
https://auckland.libguides.com/referencing-generative-ai-tools. The log is only easy if it is
kept as you go, and it is close to impossible to reconstruct later, so write the row the moment
the generation finishes.

## Nothing from this file goes on the posters

**The A3 poster brief bans AI-generated images outright.** Not "discouraged", not "cite it",
banned. Royalty-free stock is allowed if it is captioned and cited on the poster itself, but
generated imagery is not allowed at all.

That means every image on either A1 panel has to be real: a frame from the actual filmed footage,
a real photograph, a hand-drawn or vector diagram, or captioned stock. Prompt 5 and prompt 8 will
be the two most tempting frames in this set and they are both disqualified. The poster hero shots
come from the shoot.

These prompts are for the concept work, the pitch, the storyboard and the pre-visualisation of
the compositing plan. That is a real and useful job. It is just not the poster.
