# Three Te Tiriti sections, proposed

Polo answered whaanau, mahi tahi and hanga himself. He asked Claude to propose kaitiakitanga,
and left huri hanga and the Māori data question to Claude's judgement. These are drafts of the
argument, not final report prose.

**The principle running through all three: nothing here is invented to collect marks.** Every
connection below is to a decision Polo already made for his own reasons, before any of this was
written down. Where a connection is thin, it says so. The rubric asks for deep critical
reflection, and naming a limit honestly reads as reflection while overclaiming reads as
decoration.

---

## Kaitiakitanga, protection and care

### The argument

Kaitiakitanga is guardianship. The duty of care you owe something precious that is in your
keeping but is not yours to own.

Compass sees everything you make. Not the finished work, which is the part people choose to
show, but the unfinished canvas, the take that did not work, the bad take before the good one.
**That is the most private material a creative person has.** Most people will show you what
they made. Very few will show you the version before it was any good.

So a device worn specifically while making things is in possession of something precious that
is not its own. That is a kaitiakitanga position whether or not anyone names it as one.

### Compass already answers it, and the answer was never framed this way

Four rules already in the product definition turn out to be rules of care rather than features:

1. **Everything is processed on the device.** The record of what you make does not leave the
   thing on your face.
2. **Your own reference library is the only storage.** What it keeps is yours, and it keeps
   nothing else.
3. **It never shows you someone else's finished version** of what you are attempting. Read as
   a creative rule this protects the wearer from being discouraged. Read as a rule of care it
   protects *everybody else*, because the only way to show you another person's work is to
   have taken it first.
4. **Nothing on the inner face points back at the wearer.** It watches the work, not the person.

That fourth one was a product decision taken for its own reasons. It is also the clearest
statement of the position: the device's attention belongs to the thing being made.

### Where it connects to the honest negative

Polo named intellectual property as the real risk: the device sees what you are doing. His
position is that this is not necessarily negative if it is stored and secured properly.

Kaitiakitanga sharpens that. **Storing it properly is not the user's problem to solve, it is a
duty the product owes them.** A device that watches you work and then treats the record as an
asset has failed, however good its security is. That is the line between guardianship and
ownership, and it is the line Compass is built on.

### The limit, stated plainly

**Kaitiakitanga in te ao Māori is not a data policy, and borrowing it as one is a thin
reading.** It carries obligations to whenua, awa, moana, people and future generations that a pair of
glasses does not engage with at all. The honest claim is narrow: the principle gave a name and
a standard to a set of decisions already made, and that standard is higher than privacy. Care
is owed, not merely compliance.

Claiming more than that would be using the word as decoration.

## Huri hanga, diversity, plurality and transformation

### The honest part first

**The three people in the film are a narrow sample.** They are Polo, Josiah and Chloe. Two of
the three are men who work together making films, and one of them is the person who made the
project. That is not a cross section of creative Aotearoa.

The reason is a real constraint rather than an oversight: the course research rules keep
participation to friends and family, and the pool was the people who could be in the room on
the day. That constraint also chose the painter. It belongs in the record rather than being
tidied out of it.

### What the project does do

Where the project genuinely engages plurality is in **refusing a single idea of what a creative
is.** The three are a musician, a photographer and a painter, deliberately across sound, image
capture and mark making, and deliberately at beginner to moderate level rather than
professional. Compass is built for the person who makes things without it being their income.
That is a wider definition of creative practice than most tools of this kind assume.

And the interface is customisable, which came directly out of a real conversation with a
photographer: every creative works differently, so the device does not impose one way of
working.

### Transformation

Huri hanga is also transformation, and that is the project's actual argument. The position is
not that creative work survives AI unchanged. It is that **creative work changes, toward
direction and judgement, and the human stays at the centre of it.** Polo's own words: it moves
toward creative director work. The film argues for transformation rather than preservation,
which is a more honest claim and a harder one.

## Māori creators, AI, and what can honestly be said

### The data exists and it is specific

The Taiuru study found **76 per cent of Māori music creators surveyed, 40 of 53, are concerned
about AI.** That is a small base and it is drawn as 40 of 53 rather than scaled, because hiding
a small base would be worse than showing it.

It is a higher rate of concern than the general creator figure, and the concern is not only
about income. For Māori creators the material at risk includes cultural material, which is not
replaceable and is not individually owned in the way copyright assumes.

### What the project can claim

Compass's on-device rule is relevant here, and relevant more sharply than it is for anyone
else. A device that sends everything you make to a server is a different proposition for
someone whose work carries cultural material than it is for someone painting a landscape.

### What it cannot claim

**No Māori person was consulted on this project.** That is stated plainly in the mahi tahi
section and it applies here too. Compass was not designed with Māori creators, it was designed
in a context where their documented concern is part of the evidence.

So the claim is limited to this: the concern is real, it is documented, it is higher among
Māori creators than among creators generally, and the product's one storage rule speaks to it.
Anything beyond that would be speaking for people who were not in the room.
