**There is deliberately no Compass label in that legend.** Compass has no voice
in the film or in the product, and per the truth sheet's recommendation on row
A13 it makes no sound at all, not just no speech. Nothing in this script is
spoken, written or sounded by the device.

**All three actors speak to someone out of frame who is never seen and never
heard.** That is a decision, not an omission: it keeps the cast at three for a
one day shoot, it keeps every line about their own day rather than about the
product, and on a silent exhibition screen the caption carries the line anyway.

**Three standing notes that apply to every shot:**

1. **The centre of the lens is empty in every composited frame, with no
   exceptions.** So is the bottom edge, per the truth sheet's recommendation on
   A1. Both are checked shot by shot below.
2. **Third person shots cost nothing to composite.** A waveguide lens does not
   show its image to a bystander, so from outside the eyebox the prop reads as
   plain glass and no interface is built. Every third person shot in this
   script is therefore free, and that is what makes the compositing budget
   survivable.
3. **The naming card and the end cards are film cards, never lens frames.**
   Putting the product name inside the lens would put type in the centre of
   vision and break the one rule in the one shot whose whole job is to state
   it. The cards live outside the fiction of the lens.

# 1. The compositing count, said honestly

The cap is eight composited interface shots. This script lands on it two
different ways depending on how you count, and the difference matters, so here
are both numbers rather than the flattering one.

| Count | Number | Against the cap |
|---|---|---|
| Builds (separate compositing jobs) | **7** (C1 to C7) | one spare |
| Cuts on screen carrying interface | **8** (shots 2, 5, 8, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17) | exactly on it, no spare |

Shots 8 and 9 are two cuts from one setup and one continuous build, which is
why the two numbers differ.

**The reading I used is builds**, because the brief's own reason for the cap is
compositing capacity and the compositing method being unproven, and reusing a
finished build in a second cut costs almost nothing. **Card A promised one
spare and at build level it has one.** If Polo counts cuts instead, this script
is on the cap with nothing in hand, which is card C's position rather than card
A's, and he should know that before he rules item 6.

**The cheapest way to buy a spare at cut level**, if he wants one: drop shot 15
(the illustrator's 2.5s montage cut) and give its seconds to shot 17. That
takes C6 out entirely, leaves 6 builds and 7 cuts, and costs the montage one of
its three legs.

**Shooting order.** C1, the musician's lane, is built and shot first, per the
brief. It is the only interface in the film that sits on a real object that
moves, it is the only one where a tracking failure is visible in every frame,
and everything else in the film is cheap to reschedule around it.

## 2d. Music (SP-STYLE-12)

The per-segment notes are written into the script above, which is where the rule
puts them. Two decisions worth pulling out.

**The drop lands at 0:11.5, on the frame the lane arrives.** Concept A has two
candidate peaks and they want opposite things from a track: the wake at 0:11.5
is a release that eleven seconds of held tension has already bought, and the
payoff at about 0:69 is a removal that wants the track to leave. A track has one
drop. I have put it on the wake, because that is the moment the hook is paying
off and the payoff is better served by silence than by a second peak. **If Polo
reads the payoff as the film's peak instead, the whole track is rebuilt and the
hook plays without tension underneath it**, which is the version of this film I
would expect to fail.

**The honest caveat.** The ruled primary placement is a silent exhibition loop,
so every word above describes a layer that most of this film's actual viewers
will never hear. The music design is real for the played on request cut, the
presentation and the landing page, and it is worth nothing on the screen the
film was concepted for.

# 3. What this script leaves out, deliberately

Named rather than quietly skipped, because each one was a live option.

- **The statistics passage.** The record plans one and in a silent film it is
  the most efficient way to make a `problem-aware` viewer feel recognised. It is
  not in this script and it should not be until the truth sheet's section 4 is
  CONFIRMED. Four separate problems sit in there right now, including a figure
  with the wrong base written next to it and seven citations that cannot be
  stood up, and a film has even less room for a footnote than a poster does.
- **The illustrator's proportion corrections.** Row A4 is unruled, the guardrail

- **The illustrator's recommendations stack** (shading, pencil pressure, colour

- **The on device privacy story.** The project's second real differentiator and

  exactly as the brief ruled.
- **A struggle beat, a failure beat, or any frustration.** Ruled out by the

- **Any generated image anywhere in the chain.** The interface is built in After

# 6. Its honest weakness

**The first one is the concept's, and this script inherits it whole.** The
argument rests on the audience noticing an absence, and a viewer cannot tell a
deliberate emptiness from a device that has not booted yet, because the two look
identical. Nothing in the first 11.5 seconds tells them which one they are
watching. Writing it out made this worse rather than better, because on the page
shot 1 is one line of direction and eleven and a half seconds of screen time,
and there is no craft available inside it that would fix the problem: a slower
push, a better room and a better performer all make the same shot more beautiful
and none of them make it legible.

**If it has to be rescued without changing the concept**, the cheapest move is
to put the musician's line into the last four seconds of the hold instead of
into MAGIC 3. It costs the hook proportion rule, which currently passes at
zero words, and it buys a passer by a caption to read while nothing is
happening. I have not done it in this draft, because card A says zero words in
the hook and the brief is worth testing as written.

**The second is this script's own, and it is the one I would raise first.** The
musician's falling note lane is the closest the film ever comes to breaking the
one rule, and it is in the first composited shot. A lane that tells her which
key and when, with the key face lighting at the moment to press, is one reading
away from a viewer deciding the machine is playing the piece and she is the
peripheral. The script's entire defence is a directing note: her fingers do
every press, the lane never runs ahead of her hand, and if she stops the block
holds. That is a note, not a shot. Proving it on screen would take a second
vanish beat in her scene, which costs a composite and dilutes the illustrator's
payoff, so this draft accepts the risk rather than paying for it. It is worth
testing on one built frame before the shoot.

**The third is structural.** The illustrator carries 21.5 seconds of screen
time and the entire payoff, and she is the last of the three wearers to appear,
at 36.5 seconds. Anyone who leaves before then never sees the best moment in
the film. On an exhibition screen where a visitor decides in two seconds, that
is a real exposure and it is the direct consequence of ranking her third, which
card A did for good reasons.

**And one small one.** All three actors speak to someone who is never seen and
never heard. Three times in 82 seconds it reads as a deliberate choice. If a
viewer notices it as a device rather than as a texture, it reads as a film
avoiding the cost of a fourth actor, which is exactly what it is.

# 7. What it costs to shoot

Estimates against a ledger where nearly every entry is tagged as an estimate,
per PF-06.

**The day.** One shoot day, one room dressed three times, which is PF-07 clean
with no exception call. Three setups, all desk and hands by design. The
lighting is the only real scheduling problem: the musician is written at night
and the photographer needs a daylight look. **Recommendation: black the room
out and light all three as interiors, separating them by dressing and colour
temperature rather than by time of day.** That keeps the brief's ruling that
the musician's lane is built and shot first, which a daylight middle of the day
schedule would fight.

**On camera.** Three actors, real creatives, varied by age, gender and
background, consenting, none named in the report. No fourth actor: the off
frame listeners are never seen or heard.

**Props and kit.** One pair of plain black mock glasses, one copy. A digital
piano. A camera. A sketchbook and pencils, with the illustrator's page at three
stages of finish so the scene can be shot out of order. A paper sweep, a lamp
and five small objects for the photographer's table. A phone for the piano lid.
A library music licence.

**Post.** Seven composited builds in After Effects, C1 first. The reference
photograph in C3 is shot on the day or licensed, never generated.

**The ledger.** PF-04 fails, as it does for every card in the set: `skit` is
the most expensive of the ten entries in the archetype cost ledger and the
semester budget is NZD 1,000 to 1,500. PF-05 is why it proceeds anyway, and its
condition is that a founder shot day is not free and the imputed commercial day
rate gets written down next to the decision. **That number is Polo's to put in
and it belongs in the report**, where for a capstone it is a marks opportunity
rather than a cost problem. PF-08 does not fire: the glasses are a real prop
and the interface is 2D compositing, so nothing asks for a photoreal render of
hardware that does not exist.

**Actual out of pocket**, as distinct from the imputed cost: the glasses prop,
the music licence, paper and pencils, and whatever the five objects on the
photographer's table cost. Everything else is donated.
