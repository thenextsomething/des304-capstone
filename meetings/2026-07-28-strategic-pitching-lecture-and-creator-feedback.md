## The iceberg model of design value

- Most strategic design value sits below the surface; the shape of a product rarely captures it. Three layers to communicate:
  1. Superficial value (material, colour, form): what you see right away.
  2. The interactions it creates: new behaviours, participation, shifted power dynamics.
  3. The ideas and long-term impact it promotes: confidence, community, belonging.
- Worked example: a throwable cushion microphone. The form says playful; the real value is icebreaking, flattening the speaker hierarchy, more people participating.
- Consequence for the pitch: a product photo alone cannot carry intangible value. Storytelling has to do it.

## Pitch structure the lecture recommended

- Start with the why. Open with the problem, not the solution.
- Frame the question as a How Might We: actionable, solvable through design intervention.
- Show concise compelling evidence: statistics, quotes, observations, photographs, maps.
- Narrow to a specific demographic first, then expand to secondary stakeholders.
- Include a timeline or storyboard of how someone interacts with the solution, and show where it lives (place-based renders help partners judge feasibility).
- In a 3-minute pitch, speaking and presence carry more weight than slides.

## Steve Jobs iPhone pitch, lessons pulled out in class

- Minimal text, one focus per slide, mostly images, a single positioning graph.
- Simple conversational language, no jargon, clearly not reading a script.
- Strategic pauses at key moments to build expectation.
- Problem framed first (existing smartphones: not smart, not easy to use) before the reveal.
- Visuals synced tightly to the spoken words.
- Personal and human: "I've been waiting two and a half years for this."

## Hero shots

- Show the product or service AND its context of use, never just the isolated object.
- For products: one shot with callouts labelling key functions, then a second image showing the value fulfilled (people experiencing the outcome).
- Name the project, even provisionally.
- Use large images, hand-drawn is fine.
- Avoid AI-generated visuals that are geographically or contextually wrong (wrong animals, wrong campus, wrong landscape). Plan the image in your head, then prompt AI to show exactly that.
- Avoid decorative slides; a couple of brand colours or simple lines is enough. Diagrams must survive a close read, not just look polished.

## Creator feedback for Compass (photographer perspective, from Polo's notes)

What a photographer would actually want on the glasses while shooting:

- A shot checklist on the lens: which shots have been taken, which are still owed. People forget what they have already captured mid-shoot.
- A way to review the last shot just taken, without leaving the camera.
- Reference shots and moodboards on the lens: previous work, and work the client liked.
- Customisable UI: every creative works differently, so the wearer should choose what appears on the visual layer. This is a product principle, not just a photographer request.
