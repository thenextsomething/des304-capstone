## Page 3 · Abstract

Creative people in Aotearoa are being told that AI will do their work for them. Every creative
I spoke to while making this project was worried about the same thing, and the worry was not
that the work would stop being creative. It was whether they would still need to be in the
making of it at all.

Compass is a speculative product launch film for a pair of AI glasses, set in Aotearoa in 2028
and made by a fictional Auckland company, Create Labs. The glasses do one thing and refuse to
do another. They assist the person making something, and they never make anything themselves.
They only wake once you have already started.

The film is the outcome. It is 70 to 90 seconds, shot across two days, and it follows three
everyday creators, a musician, a photographer and a painter, each working with the glasses on.
It is built as a product launch because that is how real technology arrives, and the format
lets the argument sit inside something people already know how to watch.

The value of the project is not a device. It is a position. If tools like this are going to be
built for creative people, this is what they should refuse to do.

*(246 words)*

## Page 8 · The brief

The Emerging Technologies stream asked:

**"How might we use emerging technologies and speculative design to reimagine how people in
Aotearoa connect, collaborate, and create?"**

I worked in the **create** lens.

Speculative design, in Dunne and Raby's sense, is not design that solves a problem. It makes
a thing that lets people argue about a future before it arrives. Kirby calls the version that
appears in film a diegetic prototype: an object that behaves as real inside a story, so an
audience can judge it as though it existed.

Both of those are why the outcome is a film rather than a product. The point is not to sell
Compass. The point is that someone watching can say what they do and do not want from an AI
inside their own making.

## Page 9 · The questions

**The original question** was the brief's own, above. Jed told me it was too broad to design

**"How might we use speculative design to help everyday creators in Aotearoa feel what it means

Three words in it do real work. **Everyday** rules out designing for professionals only.
**Aotearoa** rules out importing a San Francisco answer. And **feel** rules out making an
argument, because the point is an experience rather than a position paper.

## Page 13 · What they told me

The thing that surprised me is that **we all felt the same.**

I had expected a range. I thought the photographer would feel differently from the painter, and
that the people closer to the tech industry would be less worried. That is not what happened.
Every creative I talked to is scared about AI.

What they are scared of is more specific than losing work. It is this: the work they do is still
creative, but do they even need to be in the creation process anymore, and what does that look
like for them. The process is being taken over at every step. Ideation. The craft. The actual
making.

That is the insight the whole project is built on, and I did not have it before I asked.

## Page 18 · What I looked at, and what I rejected

I went through every AR and smart wearable I could find.

**I rejected anything that was too "meta",** by which I mean the glasses that look like
eyewear but have no AR display in them at all. They solve the industrial design problem by
removing the thing that makes it hard.

**I rejected anything to do with robotics.** It takes the project somewhere else entirely and it
is not what creative people asked for.

What I kept was the narrow set that actually tries to put a display in front of your eye, because
those are the ones that have to answer the question I had to answer: where does the hardware go.

> IMAGE: the wearables laid out as a field, with the two rejected groups crossed through and
> the reason written beside each.

## Page 19 · What the reading changed

I read Dunne and Raby on speculative design and Kirby on diegetic prototypes. They are dry, and
I want to be honest that reading them was not enjoyable.

They did change what I wanted. Before, I was going to make a film about AI and creative work.
After, I understood that the object had to behave as if it were real, consistently, in every
frame, or the audience would not be able to judge it. That is Kirby's point and it is the reason
the interface has rules it never breaks.

You can see it in the project. The rule that nothing is ever drawn in the centre of the lens is
not a style decision. It is the object behaving the same way every time so it reads as real.

## Page 21 · The moment it became clear

It was not a single realisation. It was the conversations.

I was asking people how they felt, and at the same time showing them early concepts and asking
what they would actually want on a lens. The two halves of that met. The thing they were scared
of was being moved out of the middle of their own process, and the thing they wanted was help
that stayed out of the middle.

That is when it became clear that the product was defined by what it refuses to do rather than
by what it does.

## Page 22 · The one rule

**Compass does not create. It only assists.**

I arrived at that line myself, out of those conversations, and it is the whole project. Every
other decision is downstream of it.

It has a hard consequence that makes it real rather than a slogan: Compass cannot create even
in principle, because a human has to be making something for there to be anything to assist.
Put the glasses on and do nothing and they show you nothing.

**They only wake once you have already started.** That single behaviour is the argument.

> IMAGE: the rule set as large as the page allows. Nothing else on it.

---

## Page 23 · The three refusals

The rule produces three refusals, and they are absolute.

**It never occupies the centre of your vision.** It works at the edge of what you are doing.
The centre stays empty, always.

**It never makes anything for you.**

**It never shows you someone else's finished version** of what you are attempting. It shows you
a source photograph, a reference, or your own earlier work. Never another person's finished
answer to the thing you are attempting.

None of these were hard to commit to, because the rule came first and these follow from it. I
kept the rule at the centre of the whole design and the refusals never moved.

> IMAGE: three lens diagrams, one per refusal, each showing what does not appear.

An everyday creator is literally me, Chloe and Josiah.

We are photographers and videographers. We play the piano and we paint. All of that is actually

## Page 26 · Aotearoa, 2028

**Why 2028.** Wearables are getting genuinely good now. The one I would believe in is ready next
year or the year after, and with how fast AI is moving, 2028 is credible rather than distant.

**What the world is like.** New Zealand is slower than San Francisco, because that is where
adoption starts. But New Zealand creatives *made* Compass, and Aotearoa is the deliberate first
market before the United States, which is the normal play for a local company going overseas.

Nothing is fully automated. Everything is faster: websites, brand books, storyboards, scripts.
Speed is not the same as better, because the story and the human part still matter.

The fear is fully here. Jobs are going, but not catastrophically.

## Page 27 · Why glasses

Glasses are an easy wearable, and I do not expect anyone to wear these all the time. **They are
for creative work only.** You put them on to make something and you take them off when you are
done.

The real reason for the form, though, is the point of view. The glasses see what the user sees,
through their eyes. They do not have their own view of the world and they are not pointed at
the room. They are pointed at the work.

That is also why nothing on the inside of the frame points back at the wearer. The device's
attention belongs to the thing being made.

## Page 28 · What I did in Develop

Develop is the longest part of this project and the part that went wrong most often.

Two problems ran in parallel. **The object:** what Compass actually is, which I had to build
before I could film it. **The interface:** how to get a convincing display onto real footage,
which had been the unsolved wall since Week 7.

They are documented separately below because they failed separately.

> IMAGE: two parallel tracks, the object and the interface, with the failures marked on each.

---

## Page 29 · Prototype one: air-dried clay

I started by getting a bunch of glasses, and I went to the FabLab team and to Jaycar to work
out how to do this at all, because I had not made a physical object like this before.

The first version was **air-dried clay**, which I then oven-dried. The point was not to make
the product. It was to see how it looked and, more importantly, how it felt on a face.

**It was pretty bad.** I am saying that plainly because the point of making it was never for it
to be good. It told me the proportions, it told me the weight of the thing sitting on the brow,
and it told me that the housing had to be bigger than I wanted it to be.

> IMAGE: the clay prototype, more than one angle. This is one of the most valuable photographs
> in the report.

---

## Page 30 · Why it had to be thicker

I had been designing toward thin. Everything I liked in the reference set was thin.

Making the clay version is what ended that, and then looking harder at real AR frames confirmed
it. Compass needs cameras and sensors and a display. That hardware has to physically be
somewhere.

**It would not have been feasible for it to have no thickness to it, because where would all
the tech go.**

That is a change in direction and I want it read as a decision rather than a drift. The thin
version was a drawing of something that looks good. The thicker one is a drawing of something
that could work. The rubric for this project asks whether the solution is technologically
feasible, and the honest answer only became available once I built the thing.

> IMAGE: thin reference beside the built object, with the hardware that has to fit drawn into
> the housing.

---

## Page 31 · Prototype two: the real one

Knowing what the first version taught me, I got better glasses with the frames I actually
wanted, and **mouldable plastic from Jaycar.**

Then: moulded it around the frames, sanded it back, spray-painted it, and added the small parts
that represent where the camera and the sensors sit.

It was **a very long, hard and tedious process**, and that is most of what I learned from it.
Making a physical object well is a craft I do not have, and I now know how much time it
actually takes.

> IMAGE: the process in sequence. Frames, moulding, sanding, painting, finished. One row.

## Page 32 · What I am not happy with

I was not super happy with how the physical prototype ended up.

It was good enough for what it had to do, which was two things: be worn on camera in the film,
and be the object the Blender model was built from. It does both.

But it is not the designed object. The lens is the clearest example. I wanted a light smoke
lens you can see eyes through, and I could not source glasses with the right lens, so the
prototype has a blue mirror lens instead. The renders and the film show the lens I designed.
The prototype shows the lens I could get.

**If I did this again I would put much more into the physical prototype.** I only properly
understood that it could have been a strength of this project at my second one on one, by which
point the time to act on it had largely gone.

## Page 33 · The 3D model

With the physical object built, I modelled Compass in Blender from photographs of it, to its
real measurements. 141mm across the front, 50 degrees of wrap each side, 142mm arms, and a
housing 26mm deep.

Every part is a separate named object, so the camera, the sensors, the touch panel, the
speaker outlet and the charging contacts can each be isolated or turned to face the camera.

I then made my own changes to the model and rendered the final images from it myself.

The reason for modelling it at all is that the physical prototype could not be photographed the
way a product is photographed. The model gave me any angle I needed, consistently.

## Page 34 · The interface

> IMAGE: the UI drafts, in order, earliest to latest.

The interface went through more versions than anything else in the project, and the rules did
most of the work.

Nothing is ever drawn in the centre. Compass only marks the working band, which is where your
hands already are. For the painter it shows the reference proportion beside her line and never
marks her line as wrong, which is why the word "corrections" never appears anywhere in this
project.

The painter's interface changed late, on 28 September, when the third character became a
painter rather than an illustrator. Colour moved from a small line at the bottom of her stack
to the centre of her interface, because for a painter colour is the work.

---

## Page 35 · The compositing wall

Getting a believable display onto real footage had been unsolved since Week 7, and I tried to
solve it with AI first.

**AI was just not good enough.** When I gave it footage it could not do it. The two failures
that mattered: **small revisions and refinements were not possible**, and **consistency was not
there.** I could sometimes get one good frame. I could not get the same interface behaving the
same way across a sequence, and I could not ask for a small change and get only that change
back.

That is a specific and useful finding rather than a general complaint. It is also exactly what
I told my own editors three weeks later: AI output is a starting point, it is weak on small
revisions, and it goes repetitive without strong direction. I learned that on this project
before I said it to them.

> IMAGE: the failed attempts, side by side, with the inconsistency visible across frames.

## Page 36 · So I learned After Effects

**I learned After Effects myself, and I edited this whole film myself in After Effects.**

## Page 38 · The script and the storyboard

> IMAGE: the storyboard, as a grid, at whatever size fits.

The film is 70 to 90 seconds, three wearers intercut, roughly 60 to 70 per cent point of view

# DELIVER

## Page 39 · What I did in Deliver

The outcome is the film. Alongside it there are two A1 exhibition posters and the physical
prototype on the table.

**There is deliberately no fake website, no fake social profiles and no product page.** I
considered building the whole fake product stack and I scratched it. A launch video is both
what I would rather make and the more accurate artefact, because a launch video is how
advanced technology products actually release themselves, speculative or real. Adding a fake
Instagram would have been more stuff rather than a better argument.

> IMAGE: the three outcomes laid out. Film, posters, object.

---

## Page 40 · Why a launch film

A launch film carries an argument that a documentary or a scenario does not. It asks you to
want the thing. That is a harder test than asking you to think about it, and it is the test a
speculative product should have to pass.

My reference was the Astra GPT-6 launch video. **What I took from it: it made the person the
centre, and the product was just a tool.** That is the same relationship Compass has to the
people wearing it, so the format and the argument agree with each other.

What I refused to take is the register where everything is effortless and nobody is actually
making anything. In Compass's film the people are working, and the product is at the edge of
the frame.

# IMPACT

---

## Page 46 · What changes for one person

For a creative person in Auckland, the change is that **they still feel safe and secure in
their own creative practice.**

AI as a real tool of assistance instead of a replacement, and it does not matter whether they
are a beginner or a professional. It helps them either way.

That is a smaller claim than most technology makes and I think it is the right size. The
product does not promise to make anyone better. It promises not to take the making away from
them.

> IMAGE: a theory of change diagram. Inputs, the product's refusals, the outputs, the outcome.
> Keep the outcome modest and keep the honest limits visible on the same diagram.

---

## Page 47 · The wider impact, which is the actual claim

The bigger thing I want is not that this device sells.

**I would like this product to be what creatives look at to set the example for other tech
products, and for anything AI or anything tech that is going to assist creatives. Our
philosophy is the creatives' philosophy, and that should be representative of all products
built for creatives.**

So the impact is the philosophy spreading rather than the object spreading. If a company
building a real tool for creative people looked at Compass and took the three refusals
seriously, that is the outcome. And Create Labs would have other product lines, not only this
one.

> IMAGE: the philosophy drawn as something that moves outward from the product to other
> products, rather than a product that scales.

---

## Page 48 · The honest limits

**It has nothing to do with the environment.** This project makes no environmental claim and
I am not going to invent one. It is a wearable device, which means manufacturing and
electronic waste, and I have not addressed that.

**There is a cost barrier.** A device like this has a real barrier to entry, and the people in
this project's own research are earning a median of $19,500 from their creative work. The
product is designed for people who may not be able to afford it, and that tension is real.

**The wider impact only works if other companies carry the philosophy too.** On its own, one
product with good refusals does not change an industry.

> IMAGE: the three limits set plainly, as text. Do not dress them up.

## Page 49 · The negative impact

The obvious one is **intellectual property, and the fact that it sees what you are doing.**

My position is that this is not necessarily negative if the record is stored and secured in
the right ways, and that is why everything processes on the device and why your own reference
library is the only storage.

But I want to name it as a real risk rather than solve it in a sentence. A device worn while
you make things is in possession of the most private material a creative person has, and the
next page is about what that obligation actually is.

# CULTURAL POSITIONALITY AND TE TIRITI PARTNERSHIP

---

## Page 50 · Whaanau, and mahi tahi

**Whaanau, relationships and collective belonging.** There is collective belonging in this in
the sense that anyone wearing Compass and using it is still a creative at heart, and they are
still creating. The glasses do not work without the creator behind them. The device cannot
produce anything on its own, so the person is not optional to it. That is a belonging the
product structurally depends on rather than claims.

**Mahi tahi, collaboration and partnership.** I worked with Josiah, Chloe, Navia, Rohan and my
sister Liane, and with the FabLab team, Jed and Leo.

**I did not consult anyone Māori on this project.** I am saying that plainly rather than
leaving it to be noticed. It is a real limit on what I can claim in this section, and it sets
what I say on the next two pages.

> IMAGE: the people who were actually involved, named, with what each contributed.

---

## Page 51 · Kaitiakitanga

Kaitiakitanga is guardianship. The duty of care you owe something precious that is in your
keeping but is not yours to own.

Compass sees everything you make. Not the finished work, which is the part people choose to
show, but the unfinished canvas, the take that did not work, the bad version before the good
one. **That is the most private material a creative person has.** Most people will show you
what they made. Very few will show you the version before it was any good.

Four decisions I had already made turn out to be rules of care rather than features.
Everything processes on the device. Your own reference library is the only storage. It never
shows you someone else's finished version, which protects everybody else, because the only way
to show you another person's work is to have taken it first. And nothing on the inner face
points back at the wearer, so the device's attention belongs to the work and not to the person.

This sharpens my answer on the previous page. **Storing it properly is not the user's problem
to solve. It is a duty the product owes them.** A device that watches you work and then treats
the record as an asset has failed, however good its security is.

**The limit.** Kaitiakitanga in te ao Māori is not a data policy, and borrowing it as one is a
thin reading. It carries obligations to whenua, awa, moana, people and future generations that
a pair of glasses does not engage with at all. My claim is narrow: the principle gave a name
and a standard to decisions I had already made for other reasons, and that standard is higher
than privacy. Care is owed, not merely compliance.

> IMAGE: the four rules of care, drawn against the device.

---

## Page 52 · Hanga, and huri hanga

**Hanga, creation through purposeful action.** This is the closest of the five to the project's
own argument. Compass cannot create. It can only assist something a person has already begun,
which means every outcome it is involved in was made through someone's purposeful action. The
rule I built the product on and this principle are saying the same thing.

**Huri hanga, diversity, plurality and transformation.** The honest part first: the three
people in the film are a narrow sample. They are me, Josiah and Chloe. Two of us are men who
work together making films, and one of us made the project. That is not a cross section of
creative Aotearoa, and the reason is the course research rules keeping participation to friends
and family, plus who could actually be in the room on the day.

Where the project does engage plurality is in refusing one idea of what a creative is. Sound,
image capture and mark making, at beginner to moderate level rather than professional. And the
interface is customisable, which came directly out of Navia telling me that every creative works
differently.

On transformation: my argument is not that creative work survives AI unchanged. It is that it
changes, toward direction and judgement, and that the human stays at the centre of it. That is
a harder claim than preservation and I think it is the true one.

---

## Page 53 · Māori creators and AI

The Taiuru study found 76 per cent of Māori music creators surveyed, 40 of 53, are concerned
about AI. That is a higher rate than the general creator figure, and the concern is not only
about income. For Māori creators the material at risk includes cultural material, which is not
replaceable and is not owned in the way copyright assumes.

Compass's on-device rule is relevant here, and more sharply than it is for anyone else. A
device that sends everything you make to a server is a different proposition for someone whose
work carries cultural material than it is for someone painting a landscape.

**What I cannot claim.** Compass was not designed with Māori creators. It was designed in a
context where their documented concern is part of the evidence. The concern is real, it is
documented, it is higher among Māori creators than among creators generally, and the product's
storage rule speaks to it. Anything beyond that would be me speaking for people who were not
in the room.

# REFLECTIONS AND NEXT STEPS

---

## Page 54 · What I would do differently

**I would put much more into the physical prototype.** I only properly understood that it could
have been a strength of this project at my second one on one, and by then the time to act on it
had mostly gone. The object was always going to be on a table next to the film at the
exhibition, and I treated it as a prop for the shoot rather than as a thing in its own right.

**I would learn After Effects sooner.** I knew from Week 7 that compositing was the central
technical risk. I left learning the tool until the break, and it took longer than I expected.
Everything downstream of it, including the shoot date, moved later because of that one
decision.

---

## Page 55 · What I learned

The research finding that stayed with me is that **we all felt the same.** I went in expecting
a range of opinion across different kinds of creative people and there was not one. That
changed the project from an argument I was making into something I was reporting.

The making finding is that **AI is a starting point and not the finished work**, and I learned
that the hard way on my own footage before I said it to my own team. It is weak on small
revisions and it goes repetitive without strong direction. The thing that fixed my film was
learning a craft, not finding a better prompt.

And the unexpected one: generating previsualisation frames turned out to be the most genuinely
useful AI in the whole project, which is the same lesson again. Useful at the start. Not at the
end.

---

## Page 56 · Next steps

**Refine the actual product.** The industrial design arrived at feasibility rather than at
resolution, and there is a real version of this object to be designed.

**Put the interface on real AR glasses** and find out how it actually looks in life rather than
in a composite. Everything in this project about the display is argued from footage. The next
honest test is a real lens.
