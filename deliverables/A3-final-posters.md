## What it is

The final version of the Capstone posters, following the criteria set out in class. They must be highly visual.

## Required contents

- **Title of project**
- **Subtitle:** this should be the refined How Might We question
- **Project blurb:** a summary expressing the main issue being addressed and the value the project brings, 120 to 150 words
- **Hero shots:** at least two hero shots of the project **in the context of use**
- **More images, diagrams, and very little text** answering:
  - **Why?** Why do we care, why is this a problem that needs solving, background of the challenge, using brief research data where adequate
  - **What?** What is the proposed solution, what does it look like
  - **Who?** Who are the stakeholders most affected or benefited, who is the target audience
  - **How?** How does the proposal work, does it have steps, how would participants engage. Provide details of the main technical aspects and/or a system map or storyboard
- **Project impact:** show what will change with the project and state the main impacts
- **Your name, stream name, year, Design or Degree Programme, and a small UoA logo**

## The image rule

**AI-generated images are not allowed in the poster.**

Royalty-free stock images are allowed as part of the hero shots, with the brief giving "a family using your product" or "Auckland city in the background" as examples. Stock images must be captioned and the source cited directly on the poster, for example "Source: Getty images". The full citation goes in the visual project report.

## What this means for Compass

This is my reading of the brief against where the project currently is, not part of the brief itself.

**The no-AI-images rule is the significant one.** The project's whole storyboarding workflow runs through generative tools, and those outputs cannot appear on the poster. Every image on it has to be real: filmed footage, photographs of the physical glasses, hand-drawn or vector diagrams, or captioned stock.

**Hero shots have to show the product in use, and they are due first.** Half the marks here are hero shots, and they must show context, function or experience. For a wearable that is almost certainly a person wearing the Compass glasses mid-creative-session, with the interface visible. That means either a real photograph of the physical prototype being worn, or a still frame from finished composited footage. Either way the compositing problem, which has been the project's unsolved wall since Week 7, has to be solved to a printable standard by mid-October, at 300 dpi where flaws show far more than in motion.

**15 October is therefore the real deadline for the visual language, not 29 October.** The posters land two weeks before the prototype is due and six days before the presentation. Work backwards from 15 October, not from the end of the month.

**CMYK matters for this specific project.** The UI language is built on glowing blues and whites over dark scenes. Those are exactly the colours that shift most between screen RGB and print CMYK. Test-print early rather than discovering it in October.

**Readable from 1.5 metres pushes against the film's aesthetic.** The film is quiet, minimal and understated. A poster that has to carry Why, What, Who, How and Impact legibly from across a room is a different discipline. Treat them as separate design problems that share an identity, not as the film flattened onto paper.

**The subtitle is already written.** The refined HMW is settled: how might we use speculative design to help everyday creators in Aotearoa feel what it means to work alongside AI, rather than be replaced by it.
