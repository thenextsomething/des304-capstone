## The headline

Polo went in saying the launch film would still be the final outcome. The tutors did not disagree with that, but pushed hard for a physical prototype to sit alongside it. The outcome Polo settled on: **the film stays the main deliverable, and a physical interface prototype gets built on AR goggles the university already has, rather than fabricating a device.**

## Why they pushed for an object

The strongest argument was about the exhibition audience:

> "Put yourself in the perspective of a viewer or someone who attends your presentation. If you don't give them any experience of what it would be like to wear those glasses, there might be a bit of disappointment. If you've just showed them the film, they might feel like they're not so convinced. The video would have to be amazing. They would still want to see or experience it. Especially if they're wandering around in that exhibition context."

And on format specifically. Polo asked whether the prototype has to be physical:

> "No, it needs to be some kind of prototype. So it could be a film, it could be a poster. It could be a mix of different things."

Followed immediately by:

> "I mean, if your whole project is about the glasses that someone puts on, I think there needs to be some sort of physical object. Otherwise it's going to be... where's the thing?"

So the course does not formally require a physical object, but for this project the tutors think it does.

Their conclusion: **"this is where the film and the object combined give you the best of both worlds. And then you just give a taster, some functionality."**

## The two routes they named

1. Design what it will look like, without functionality.
2. Use goggles the university already has, and actually display the interface on them.

Route 2 is what Polo is taking.

## On whether building it now breaks the future setting

Polo raised the obvious objection: the project is set in a near future, so does making a working version now undermine that?

> "Well, the future is tomorrow, which is next week."

And, importantly for scope:

> "Just because you make it a functional prototype doesn't mean that it does everything. There can still be some trickery or whatever. It's just the idea that you're giving something to people that they can put on. And maybe it is playing the video at some point."

This removes the pressure to build something that genuinely works. A taste of the wearer's view is enough.

## On not giving up too early

The most pointed piece of advice:

> "It's very important that you don't give up on this too easily without seeking help and support that you have available to you, which, after you leave here, you won't have."

They named a person to talk to about making it functional, and told Polo to go and see him **immediately after the session**. The name in the transcript appears as both "Mark" and "Aldo" at different points, and Polo confirmed afterwards that the person is **Aldo Visini** (aldo.visini@auckland.ac.nz). One of them was described as being out of the country a lot of the time, which is a reason not to sit on the introduction.

They also noted that if a more advanced or refined model is wanted later, that can come further down the road. First step is a conversation.

## Technical concern raised

Polo's worry is not display, it is detection:

> "It'll be easier to display whatever on the goggles, but it's more so the detection."

Tutors' response: the detection may not actually be the problem, there are ways round it, and it is not something to solve alone.

## User research

Polo reported talking to **Navia**, a photographer, asking what she would want to see on the lens if she wore the glasses. He plans the same conversations with a beginner sketcher, a videographer, and a beginner piano player, so that the interface is accurate to each person's real point of view.

Note: the videographer is new. The film's three wearers are still the pianist, photographer and illustrator, so it is unclear whether the videographer is a fourth character or simply another person to learn from. Worth settling.

Tutors reminded him again to **keep this research to friends and family**, consistent with the course rules on research with human subjects.
