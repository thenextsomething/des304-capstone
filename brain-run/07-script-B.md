**The film is silent by default.** Ruled placement is an exhibition screen

never overlaps a point-of-view interface panel. Nothing in this film means

**The grammar, stated once because it is the concept.** Every single cut in this
film is a hand matched to a hand: position, direction and speed carried across
the cut so three rooms read as one continuous gesture. Nobody meets. The edit
insists they are doing the same thing anyway. Where a shot below ends on a hand
leaving frame, the next shot begins on a hand entering it on the same line and
at the same speed, and the storyboard has to carry that match or the shot is
worthless.

## The 15-second exhibition loop

Card B's strongest single asset and the reason the cut order matters. **The loop
is shots 1 to 10 exactly, 0.0 to 14.0s, plus one second of the musician's lane
from shot 12 to close the seam.** No recutting, no separate assembly: the first
fifteen seconds of the film already are the loop. It plays silent, it plays
seamlessly because the last frame is a black card and the first frame is a black
key, and it is the only version of this film where a passer-by across a room
gets hands, three wakes, three faces and the product name inside fifteen
seconds.

This is also, as it happens, the only real answer this film has to its own
first-guess problem, and the closing section below is honest about how partial
that answer is.

## The closing section: what this script breaks, leans on, and costs

### Brain rules this script breaks, and why

- **CP-LAUNCH-05, hard.** The base concept does not land in the first seconds.
  For four full seconds a cold viewer sees hands and no product. The brief's
  answer to CP-LAUNCH-05 was that the object carries it, because the glasses go
  on in frame one, and this concept removes exactly that. Broken knowingly, and
  it is the concept's price of entry.
- **CP-LAUNCH-07's first-guess test.** Dealt with at length below.
- **SP-LAUNCH-01.** PROBLEM is deleted rather than collapsed. This is a
  set-level break owned by the project's no-struggle ruling and the minimal
  before rule, not by this concept.
- **FP-14 and FP-21.** The villain never appears and the negative is never
  staged. What carries it is the absence itself, held for thirteen seconds at
  the payoff. That is the brief's item 2 divergence, taken as written.
- **SP-BUDGET-04's segment count.** At 82 seconds the rule implies eight to ten
  segments of eight to ten seconds. This film has seven named segments and three
  of them run fourteen to sixteen seconds. The rule's own instruction when it
  gets tight is to cut segments rather than compress them, and this concept
  cannot cut a wearer. I have not compressed anything to hide it; the three long
  MAGIC segments are long and the document says so.
- **SP-STYLE-05's segment naming.** The card named a separate "THE PAYOFF"
  segment, which is not a name the launch structure carries. I have filed it as
  MAGIC 4 so the header comes from the structure file, which is what the rule
  requires, and the card's intent is unchanged.
- **SP-HOOK-05's floor, arguably.** The viewer knows what space they are in by
  the end of the first segment only if you count the wake at 4.0s as inside the
  hook. On this cut the wake is the first beat of ENCAPSULATE, so on a strict
  reading the floor is missed by three seconds.
- **The truth gate itself.** Step 7 is hard-blocked on the PENDING fiction rows
  and this draft was written anyway, on the same authorisation that ran steps 4
  and 5 against a DRAFT brief. Every affected beat is flagged inline rather than
  quietly resolved.

### Its honest weakness

The card's own self-critique is right and writing it did not make it wrong. It
is the best-made film in the set and the thinnest argument in it. Strip the
match cuts out and there is no film left, only three competent product
demonstrations in a row. Everything that makes the thing watchable is craft
sitting on top of a structural idea that is really a style decision, and the
test CP-LAUNCH-01 applies is exactly that: a launch leads with the villain, the
vision or a wow statement, and this one leads with an edit.

Drafting it surfaced a second weakness the card did not name. **The hand grammar
and the compositing cap pull in opposite directions.** Hand-matched cutting wants
cuts, and cuts inside a point-of-view segment are composited shots. The only way
to hold seven was the discipline stated in the tail, that every MAGIC segment
gets exactly one interface shot and every other shot in the segment is framed to
keep the interface out. That works, and it is defensible, but notice what it
costs: the film's binding device and the film's product are almost never in the
same frame. For three quarters of the running time the hands are doing the
cutting and Compass is nowhere in sight. A film whose concept is "the cut logic
and the product logic are the same logic" spends most of its length unable to
show both at once.

And the third one, which is the marker's read and is the reason the concept pack
recommended cutting this card: a report graded on process has to explain in
words what the film does, and "the binding device is a match cut" reads as
technique rather than argument in that document. Nothing in the writing fixes
that, because it is true.

### Did the writing solve the first-guess problem?

**No. It reduced it, in one specific placement, and that is all.**

The honest measurement. At four seconds a cold viewer's guess is "a creative
software ad" or "an art supplies ad", exactly as the card predicted. The writing
moves three things and no more: the wake lands at 4.0s rather than later, so the
guess is wrong for four seconds instead of eight; the glasses are on three faces
by 12.4s; and the product is named at 14.0s. So the category becomes legible at
about five seconds and named at fourteen. Against AL-ALL-03's three to six
second hook window that is late but survivable, and against the ruled placement,
an exhibition screen where a visitor decides in about two seconds, it is not
survivable at all.

The one real mitigation is structural rather than written, and it belongs to the
loop. The exhibition cut is the first fifteen seconds on repeat, and a loop has
no first frame. A visitor who arrives at any point in it sees the wake, the
faces and the product name within at most ten seconds of arriving, because those
events occur once every fifteen. That genuinely converts a "first four seconds"
problem into an "any four seconds" problem, and on the placement the project
actually ruled, that is a real answer. It does nothing whatsoever for the
82-second film played linearly in a presentation or on the landing page, where
the four seconds are the four seconds.

What I want to be clear about is that this is not a writing failure that a
better draft would fix. The only line-level fix available is to put the glasses
in frame one, and a film that opens on the glasses is card A. Concept B is
defined by refusing that. So the correct reading is not "the script did not
solve it" but "the concept spends CP-LAUNCH-05 and CP-LAUNCH-07 deliberately,
and the exhibition loop buys back about two thirds of the price". Whether that
trade is worth making is step 6's call and Polo's, not this document's.

### What it would cost to shoot

**Cash, against the semester budget of NZD 1,000 to 1,500.** One day, one
location dressed three times, three donated setups, three friends as cast, one
pair of plain black mock glasses, a library music licence. PF-07 is clean:
everything in this film is desk and hands, which is why the illustrator won the
third slot in the first place. PF-08 does not fire: the glasses are a real prop
and every interface frame is 2D compositing in After Effects, not a photoreal
render of hardware that does not exist.

The one thing this concept adds that the others do not is a **macro lens**, and
it is not optional. The hand grammar is the concept, and it lives or dies on
being able to get properly close on a thumb, a key and a pencil tip. Borrowed if
possible; hire if not, at roughly NZD 80 to 150 for the day, which is a
shopping-list problem rather than a budget problem. That is an estimate, not a
quote.

**The real cost is in the edit, not the shoot.** Hand-matched cutting is a
precision job and it cannot be discovered in the edit: the material has to be
shot with every match already planned, at matched speed and matched direction,
which means the storyboard for this concept has to be tighter than for any other
card in the set and the shoot day is less forgiving. There is no "we will find
it in the cut" available here. Budget the storyboard time, not the edit time.

**The imputed commercial cost, written down because PF-05 requires it and
because a capstone report earns marks for saying it.** On the brain's own cost
table, a standard TNS shoot day with lighting runs NZD 1,100 to 1,150, and an
editor and motion designer runs NZD 400 to 800 per project. So one day plus the
edit is roughly **NZD 1,500 to 1,950 at TNS's own rates**, before two things
that are not on the table at all: talent, which TNS has never costed because no
skit has ever bought a performance, and the seven composited interface shots,
which sit well outside what the editor rate assumes a project contains. A fair
statement for the report is that this film would cost TNS somewhere upward of
two thousand dollars to make commercially and is being made for the price of a
lens hire, and every one of those numbers is an estimate against a ledger where
nearly every entry is tagged as one. Said out loud per PF-06, which is the rule
that asks for exactly this sentence.

**The scheduling order, unchanged from the brief.** The musician's lane is shot
first, because it is the hardest composite in the film and everything else is
cheap to reschedule.
