## Page 3 · Abstract

*(Unchanged from draft one. 246 words.)*

Creative people in Aotearoa are being told that AI will do their work for them. Every creative I
spoke to while making this project was worried about the same thing. The worry was not that the
work would stop being creative. It was whether they would still need to be in the making of it
at all.

Compass is a speculative product launch film for a pair of AI glasses. It is set in Aotearoa in
2028 and made by a fictional Auckland company called Create Labs. The glasses do one thing and
refuse to do another. They assist the person making something and they never make anything
themselves. They only wake once you have already started.

The film is the outcome. It runs 70 to 90 seconds and was shot across two days. It follows three
everyday creators. A musician, a photographer and a painter, each working with the glasses on.
It is built as a product launch because that is how real technology arrives, and the format lets
the argument sit inside something people already know how to watch.

The value of the project is not a device. It is a position. If tools like this are going to be
built for creative people then this is what they should refuse to do.

## Page 9 · The brief

The Emerging Technologies stream asked: **how might we use emerging technologies and speculative
design to reimagine how people in Aotearoa connect, collaborate, and create?**

I worked in the **create** lens.

Speculative design in Dunne and Raby's sense is not design that solves a problem. It makes a
thing that lets people argue about a future before it arrives. Kirby calls the version that
appears in film a diegetic prototype. An object that behaves as real inside a story so an
audience can judge it as though it existed.

Both of those are why the outcome is a film rather than a product. The point is not to sell
Compass. The point is that someone watching can say what they do and do not want from an AI
inside their own making.

## Page 12 · The tools I used to think with · NEW

The methodology gave the structure. These are the tools that did the actual work inside it.

**Mind mapping.** Used at the very start, branching out from my own interests. It showed me what
was possible. It did not show me what was worth doing, and a single conversation did that instead.

**The reverse brief.** A class tool from Week 8. Framing determines outcomes, so name your
assumptions and test them and then defend your scope. It is what forced me to say out loud that
the film was the deliverable rather than the product.

**The passion test.** The question of whether I actually wanted to spend six months on a thing.
It killed one of my three concepts outright and I have no regrets about it.

**Informal conversations.** Five creative people, one at a time, asked how they feel and what
they would want. Not formal interviews, which is what the course allows at this level. This
produced the project's central insight and four of the product's features.

**Precedent mapping.** Every AR and smart wearable I could find, sorted by whether it actually
tries to put a display in front of your eye. It is what told me the thin direction was not real.

**The prompt intermediary workflow.** My own, built in DES303. Describe it to Claude in plain
language, have Claude write the prompt, then take that into the generator. The quality difference
was large and I still use it outside university.

**The Integrated Reflective Cycle.** The reflective structure from Week 4 on.

## Page 15 · What they told me

The thing that surprised me is that **we all felt the same.**

I had expected a range. I thought the photographer would feel differently from the painter. I

## Page 17 · Five requests, five features · NEW

Navia did not describe a product. She described her working day and what was missing from it.
Every one of the five things she asked for is in Compass.

| She asked for | It became |
|---|---|
| Reference images | The reference layer, top left on the lens |
| The last photo she took | The last shot, right side, in the signal colour |
| Help with f-stop and a couple of the other settings | The settings panel, left side, always there |
| A checklist | The shot checklist, top right. Her client's brief, which Compass only ticks off and never writes |
| The interface to change depending on the situation | A customisable interface, which became a world rule |

The checklist is worth stopping on. **Compass only ticks items off a list a human wrote.** A list
Compass wrote would be Compass making something and the one rule would break on screen. A list
it only tracks is assistance in its purest form. That distinction came out of her request and
not out of my head.

## Page 22 · What I looked at, and what I rejected

I went through every AR and smart wearable I could find.

**I rejected anything that was too meta**, by which I mean the glasses that look like eyewear but
have no AR display in them at all. They solve the industrial design problem by removing the thing
that makes it hard.

What I kept was the narrow set that actually tries to put a display in front of your eye. Those
are the ones that have to answer the question I had to answer. Where does the hardware go.

## Page 23 · What the reading changed

I read Dunne and Raby on speculative design and Kirby on diegetic prototypes. They are dry and I
want to be honest that reading them was not enjoyable.

They did change what I wanted. Before, I was going to make a film about AI and creative work.
After, I understood that the object had to behave as if it were real, consistently, in every
frame, or the audience would not be able to judge it. That is Kirby's point and it is the reason
the interface has rules it never breaks.

You can see it in the project. The rule that nothing is ever drawn in the centre of the lens is
not a style decision. It is the object behaving the same way every time so it reads as real.

## Page 27 · The one rule

**Compass does not create. It only assists.**

I arrived at that line myself out of those conversations. It is the whole project. Every other
decision is downstream of it.

It has a hard consequence that makes it real rather than a slogan. Compass cannot create even in
principle, because a human has to be making something for there to be anything to assist. Put
the glasses on and do nothing and they show you nothing.

**They only wake once you have already started.** That single behaviour is the argument.

> IMAGE None. Set the rule as large as the page allows and put nothing else on it.

## Page 28 · The three refusals

The rule produces three refusals and they are absolute.

**It never occupies the centre of your vision.** It works at the edge of what you are doing. The
centre stays empty, always.

**It never makes anything for you.**

**It never shows you someone else's finished version** of what you are attempting. It shows you a
source photograph or a reference or your own earlier work.

None of these were hard to commit to, because the rule came first and these follow from it.

> IMAGE: three lens diagrams, one per refusal, each showing what does not appear.

## Page 30 · Who else this affects · NEW

Compass is worn by one person. It is not only about them.

**The client.** A creative using Compass is almost always making something for somebody. A brief,
a job, a commission. Navia's work is project to project and the checklist in the film is literally
her client's brief. So the chain runs: Compass assists the creative, the creative does the work,
and **the client gets a better outcome.** The person who benefits second never wears the glasses.

**Every other creative.** This one is less obvious and it matters more. The third refusal, never
showing you someone else's finished version, is not protection for the wearer. It is protection
for everybody else, because the only way to show you another person's work is to have taken it
first. A tool that refuses to do that is making a choice about people who will never use it.

**Studios and teams.** If the tool assumes a person stays in the making, the people who hire
creatives keep hiring creatives. If it assumes the opposite, they do not. That is the argument
underneath the whole film and it is the one I am living at work.

## Page 32 · Aotearoa, 2028

**Why 2028.** Wearables are getting genuinely good now. The one I would believe in is ready next
year or the year after. With how fast AI is moving, 2028 is credible rather than distant.

**What the world is like.** New Zealand is slower than San Francisco, because that is where

## Page 33 · Why glasses

Glasses are an easy wearable and I do not expect anyone to wear these all the time. **They are
for creative work only.** You put them on to make something and you take them off when you are
done.

The real reason for the form is the point of view. The glasses see what the user sees, through
their eyes. They do not have their own view of the world and they are not pointed at the room.
They are pointed at the work.

That is also why nothing on the inside of the frame points back at the wearer.

Develop ran across both semesters and it is the part that went wrong most often.

Two problems ran in parallel. **The object:** what Compass actually is, which had to exist before
it could be filmed. **The interface:** getting a convincing display onto real footage, which was
the unsolved wall from the moment it was first attempted.

They failed separately and they are documented separately.

## Page 35 · Ten experiments, 1 to 5 · NEW

**1. Learning Claude Code.** Built a simple assignment tracker, deliberately unrelated to the
capstone, to get an honest read on the tool. *Lesson: think architecturally before you prompt.
Vagueness fails.*

**2. AI image generation.** Natural-language description produced outputs that were way off.
*Discovery: write the prompt with Claude first, then take it into the generator. The difference
was large.* This became the prompt intermediary workflow I used all semester and still use.

**3. The storyboarding workflow.** Plain language, Claude refines, then into the generation tool.
Better every single time. *Still in use outside the course.*

**4. AI video generation.** Higgsfield, Kling, Gemini, on the Compass concept. Vague prompts gave
confident-looking noise. *The winning formula: specific physical setup, persistent interface,
raw unaltered environment. The outputs were still not good, but they clarified what the film
needed.*

**5. Suno for music.** Failed on both goals. Continuing an existing piece is unsupported, and
cinematic motion-graphic sound never arrived after hours of trying. *A dead end, and recorded as
one.*

> IMAGE: five small panels in a row, one per experiment, showing the actual output including the
> bad ones.

## Page 36 · Ten experiments, 6 to 10 · NEW

**6. The piano scenario, and the Python and OpenCV composite.** The most important one. It
produced the interface language: glowing keys just before you need them, floating note names, a
timing pulse, an edge progress bar. The script ran on real piano footage. *Partially successful.
Some interface elements dropped out of frame and it was never clean enough for a final film.*
It also produced the clearest sentence in the project: **the device does not perform, it prompts.**

**7. Wearable product landscape research.** Mapped against Frame, Ray-Ban Meta, Xreal, Spectacles.
*Direction at the time: closest to Frame. That conclusion was later overturned by actually
building the object, which is on page 39.*

**8. Compositing on live footage.** Shot a headset while painting and while photographing. Full
AI generation gave no control. AI compositing onto real footage broke believability, which a fake
launch film cannot afford. *This is the central unsolved problem and it stayed unsolved for
months.*

**9. The MUSE game, V1 to V2.** Built, received well, then judged too short and too mechanically
limited to carry the concept. *Cut from the capstone on 31 July.*

**10. The Aotearoa Creative journalism prototype.** Built in Claude Code and then deliberately
killed at Crit 2.

## Page 38 · Prototype one, air-dried clay

I started by getting a bunch of glasses. Then I went to the FabLab team and to Jaycar to work out
how to do this at all, because I had not made a physical object like this before.

The first version was **air-dried clay**, which I then oven-dried. The point was not to make the
product. It was to see how it looked and more importantly how it felt on a face.

**It was pretty bad.** I am saying that plainly because the point of making it was never for it
to be good. It told me the proportions. It told me the weight of the thing sitting on the brow.
And it told me that the housing had to be bigger than I wanted it to be.

> IMAGE: the clay prototype, more than one angle. The most valuable photograph in the report.
> Do not clean it up.

## Page 39 · Why it had to be thicker

I had been designing toward thin. Experiment 7 had put Compass closest to Brilliant Labs Frame
and everything I liked in the reference set was thin.

Making the clay version is what ended that. Then looking harder at real AR frames confirmed it.
Compass needs cameras and sensors and a display. That hardware has to physically be somewhere.

**It would not have been feasible for it to have no thickness to it, because where would all the
tech go.**

I want this read as a decision rather than a drift. The thin version was a drawing of something
that looks good. The thicker one is a drawing of something that could work. **The rubric asks
whether the solution is technologically feasible, and the honest answer only became available
once I built the thing.** That is the strongest argument I have for building it at all.

> IMAGE: a thin reference beside the built object, with the hardware that has to fit drawn into
> the housing.

## Page 40 · Prototype two, the real one

Knowing what the first version taught me, I got better glasses with the frames I actually wanted,
and **mouldable plastic from Jaycar.**

Then: moulded it around the frames. Sanded it back. Spray-painted it. Added the small parts that
represent where the camera and the sensors sit.

It was **a very long, hard and tedious process** and that is most of what I learned from it.
Making a physical object well is a craft I do not have, and I now know how much time it takes.

> IMAGE: the process in sequence, one row. Frames, moulding, sanding, painting, finished.

## Page 41 · What I am not happy with

I was not super happy with how it ended up.

It was good enough for what it had to do, which was two things. Be worn on camera in the film,
and be the object the Blender model was built from. It does both.

But it is not the designed object. The lens is the clearest example. I wanted a light smoke lens
you can see eyes through and I could not source glasses with the right lens, so the prototype has
a blue mirror lens instead. The renders and the film show the lens I designed. The prototype
shows the lens I could get.

**If I did this again I would put much more into the physical prototype.** I only properly
understood that it could have been a strength of this project at my second one on one, by which
point the time to act on it had largely gone.

## Page 42 · The 3D model

With the physical object built, I modelled Compass in Blender from photographs of it, to its real
measurements. 141mm across the front. 50 degrees of wrap each side. 142mm arms. A housing 26mm
deep.

Every part is a separate named object, so each component can be isolated or turned to face the
camera. I then made my own changes to the model and rendered the final images from it myself.

The reason for modelling it at all is that the physical prototype could not be photographed the
way a product is photographed. The model gave me any angle I needed, consistently.

## Page 43 · The interface, and the day it changed

The interface went through more versions than anything else and the rules did most of the work.
Nothing is ever drawn in the centre. Compass only marks the working band where your hands already
are.

**On 16 September the whole visual language changed, and it is a bigger move than it sounds.**
Until that day the interface was sparse warm-white marks. No panels, no borders, nothing that
read as machine-like. It became translucent glass panels with a soft blur and thin light edges.
Denser, more legible, more obviously an interface.

**It cost me an argument.** The project had been saying Compass was defined against Even
Realities' green dot-matrix readout, and this change moves Compass toward the dense panelled look
that argument was defined against. I could not tidy it, I had to drop it. What replaces it has to
rest on *when* Compass appears and *what it refuses to do* rather than on how little it draws.

That is a better argument anyway. It just cost me the easy one.

**One more late change.** On 28 September the third character became a painter rather than an
illustrator, and colour moved from a small line at the bottom of her stack to the centre of her
interface, because for a painter colour is the work.

> IMAGE: the interface before and after 16 September, side by side. This pairing is the single
> clearest piece of iteration evidence in the project.

## Page 44 · The compositing wall

I tried to solve it with AI first, across both semesters.

**AI was just not good enough.** When I gave it footage it could not do it. The two failures that
mattered: **small revisions and refinements were not possible**, and **consistency was not there.**
I could sometimes get one good frame. I could not get the same interface behaving the same way
across a sequence, and I could not ask for a small change and get only that change back.

That is a specific and useful finding rather than a general complaint. It is also exactly what I
told my own editors three weeks later. AI output is a starting point, it is weak on small
revisions, and it goes repetitive without strong direction. **I learned that on this project
before I said it to them.**

> IMAGE: the failed attempts side by side, big enough that the inconsistency actually reads.

## Page 45 · So I learned After Effects

**I learned After Effects myself, and I edited this whole film myself in After Effects.**

That was the answer to the wall. Not a better prompt. The compositing is drawn and placed by

It took longer than I expected, and **starting it sooner is the main thing I would change about

## Page 49 · Why a launch film

A launch film carries an argument that a documentary or a scenario does not. It asks you to want
the thing. That is a harder test than asking you to think about it, and it is the test a
speculative product should have to pass.

My reference was the Astra GPT-6 launch video. **What I took from it: it made the person the
centre, and the product was just a tool.** That is the same relationship Compass has to the
people wearing it, so the format and the argument agree with each other.

What I refused to take is the register where everything is effortless and nobody is actually
making anything. In Compass's film the people are working and the product is at the edge of the
frame.

> IMAGE: a reference frame beside the equivalent frame from this film.

## Page 50 · How Compass actually works · NEW

Seven steps, and it finishes where it started.

1. **You start making something.** Nothing happens before this. The device is asleep.
2. **Compass wakes.** Only now. It has no idle state to speak of.
3. **It sees what you see.** Through your eyes, pointed at the work and never at the room.
4. **It checks what it sees against your own reference library.** On the device. Nothing leaves.
5. **It marks the working band**, the strip low in the lens where your hands already are. Never
   the centre.
6. **You decide.** It has not done anything. It has put something beside you.
7. **You stop, and it fades.** Back to step one.

**And three branches that go nowhere, on purpose.** It cannot make the thing for you. It cannot
show you another person's finished version. It cannot put anything in the centre. Those are not
features that were left out. They are the design.

> system diagram the brief asks for under How, and it is also the suggestion loop that appears
> on poster 2, so draw it once and use it twice.

## Page 54 · The product

A single wraparound shield lens in light smoke, rimless along the bottom, with a gloss black
sensor housing across the brow. A small camera at the centre and a larger sensor at each end.

The AR display works to the side of vision, never the centre. The arms carry a touch bar, and
Compass is voice activated, so you can talk to it and it gives you recommendations.

Everything is processed on the device. Your own reference library is the only storage.

Nothing on the inner face points back at the wearer.

## Page 55 · The identity · NEW

Create Labs and Compass had no identity until 8 October. It is built now and it is built from
the product's own rules rather than from taste.

**The mark is an open ring with one mark at its edge, in the signal colour, never in the centre.**
It carries three of the project's rules at once. The empty centre. The single signal colour. And
a compass needle, which points without moving you.

**Colour.** Warm white #FAF4EA is the ground and the interface's own base. Ink #16181D is all
type, taken off the product's gloss black housing. Signal #6E8FC7 is the one active thing on
screen. **Signal deep #3D5A8A** was added because #6E8FC7 measures about 2.4 to 1 on warm white,
which fails readable contrast, so it cannot carry type or print small.

**The rule, and it is the product's own.** On the lens, blue marks the one element Compass is
actively offering and everything else is warm white. The identity does the same. The blue appears
once, on one thing, and never twice on a surface.

**Create Labs never gets the mark.** It is set in type alone and always smaller than the product.
The company does not announce itself.

> identity canvas has all of it.

## Page 57 · What changes for one person

For a creative person in Auckland, the change is that **they still feel safe and secure in their
own creative practice.**

AI as a real tool of assistance instead of a replacement, and it does not matter whether they are
a beginner or a professional. It helps them either way.

That is a smaller claim than most technology makes and I think it is the right size. The product
does not promise to make anyone better. It promises not to take the making away from them.

> modest and the honest limits visible on the same diagram.

## Page 58 · The wider impact

The bigger thing I want is not that this device sells.

**I would like this product to be what creatives look at to set the example for other tech
products, and for anything AI or anything tech that is going to assist creatives. Our philosophy
is the creatives' philosophy, and that should be representative of all products built for
creatives.**

So the impact is the philosophy spreading rather than the object spreading. If a company building
a real tool for creative people looked at Compass and took the three refusals seriously then that
is the outcome. And Create Labs would have other product lines, not only this one.

> IMAGE: the philosophy moving outward from the product to other products, rather than a product
> that scales.

## Page 59 · Is it feasible, and is it viable · NEW

The Innovation criterion asks three things and the report should answer all three plainly.

**Does it exist here.** No. Nothing like this is being made in Aotearoa. The wearables that exist
are either general-purpose consumer devices or enterprise tools, and none of them is built around
a creative process or around a refusal to make anything.

**Is it technologically feasible.** Yes, and the honest version of that answer only arrived by
building the object. The thin version was not feasible because the hardware has nowhere to go.
The thicker housing is. Everything Compass does is recognition and retrieval against your own
library rather than generation, which is the less demanding half of the problem.

**Is it economically viable.** Partly, and this is where I am least confident. A device at this
level is priced like a camera body rather than like a phone accessory, and the people in my own
research earn a median of $19,500 from their creative work. The honest answer is that it is
viable for the commercial end of the market first, which is where Navia sits, and that it is not
viable for the beginner the product is also designed for. That tension is real and I have not
solved it.

> IMAGE: the three questions as three plain statements with the honest answer under each.
> The third one should not be dressed up.

---

## Page 60 · The honest limits, and the negative impact · MERGED

**It has nothing to do with the environment.** This project makes no environmental claim and I am
not going to invent one. It is a wearable device, which means manufacturing and electronic waste,
and I have not addressed that.

**The wider impact only works if other companies carry the philosophy too.** On its own, one
product with good refusals does not change an industry.

**And the negative one is intellectual property, because it sees what you are doing.** My position
is that this is not necessarily negative if the record is stored and secured in the right ways,
which is why everything processes on the device and your own reference library is the only
storage. But I want to name it as a real risk rather than solve it in a sentence. A device worn
while you make things is in possession of the most private material a creative person has, and
the next page is about what that obligation actually is.

> IMAGE None. Set these plainly as text. The honesty is the point.

## Page 65 · What I would do differently, and what I learned · MERGED

**I would put much more into the physical prototype.** I only properly understood that it could
have been a strength at my second one on one, and by then the time had mostly gone. I treated it
as a prop for the shoot rather than a thing in its own right.

**I would learn After Effects sooner.** I knew compositing was the central risk from the first
failed attempt and I left the tool until the break. Everything downstream of it moved later.

**The research finding that stayed with me is that we all felt the same.** I went in expecting a

into something I was reporting.

**The making finding is that AI is a starting point and not the finished work**, learned the hard
way on my own footage before I said it to my own team. What fixed my film was learning a craft,
not finding a better prompt. And the unexpected one, that generating previsualisation frames was
the most useful AI in the whole project, is the same lesson again. Useful at the start. Not at
the end.
