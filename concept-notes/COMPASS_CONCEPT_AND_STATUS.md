## 1. What This Document Is

This is the definitive reference for Compass, the final chosen concept for Polo Umali's capstone project. Polo has completed DES303 (Design Research Practice) at the University of Auckland and is heading into DES304 (the capstone project course) next semester. The final concept, confirmed through two crit sessions, the final presentation, and a full semester of experimentation, is:

**A speculative product launch film for Compass, a fictional wearable AI device built for creatives, set in a near-future Aotearoa.**

The film is the deliverable. The product is fictional. The film needs to look and feel like it genuinely came from that future, in the vein of an Apple launch film. A secondary supporting artifact, the MUSE interactive game, exists alongside it and is covered in Section 8.

## 2. The Product: Compass

### What it is
Compass is a wearable AI device (glasses or a light headset) built specifically around the creative process. It sits in the space of the Apple Vision Pro but is designed for creativity rather than productivity. It sees what the wearer is working on, stays ambient, and offers help when they are stuck, without ever taking over.

### The core distinction (the heart of the entire project)
**It does not create. It only assists.**

The wearer puts it on when they are doing creative work and takes it off when they are done. The AI is not the creative. The human still is. It just means you never have to be alone in the process if you do not want to be. The device notices when you are stuck and offers something when it might help. It prompts, it does not perform.

Compass suggestions are framed as whispers, not instructions.

### Naming history
The product went through naming stages. Early on it had no name. It was then called MUSE for a period (Weeks 8-10, and the interactive game still carries the MUSE name). The name **Compass** was chosen during a prompt-writing session for the launch film and became the settled product name by the final presentation. Note: in some course documents and the Week 9 and 10 blogs, the wearable is referred to as MUSE. They are the same product. Going forward, the wearable is Compass and MUSE refers to the AI character in the interactive game.

### Industrial design direction
Mapped against the real wearable landscape:
- **Brilliant Labs Frame**: closest reference. Minimal, design-forward, AI-integrated without looking like tech. Compass sits closest to Frame.
- **Meta Ray-Ban smart glasses**: reference for how a consumer wearable disappears into something people actually want to wear.
- **Xreal**: the more immersive end of the spectrum, the far end from Compass.
- **Snap Spectacles**: reference for how a creative-first product gets positioned visually.

Direction: **lighter, less imposing, something that signals collaboration rather than dominance.** Closer to Frame than to Vision Pro.

The Week 9 crit group pushed Polo directly on this: before committing to a production approach, design the physical product first. What does it look like? How would it feel to wear? Even if a functioning device is never made, the object design has to be real enough that the film is believable. This reframed the film from a filming-and-compositing problem into a product design problem first, then a filming problem.

### The UI design language (developed through the piano experiment)
The clearest articulation of what Compass does came from prototyping a scenario of a pianist learning a piece with the wearable guiding them in real time. The overlay had to reflect ambient intelligence, not replicate a screen-based tool strapped to someone's face. The wearable knows what your hands are doing, knows where you are in the piece, can anticipate what is coming.

What was landed on for the piano scenario:
- Glowing keys appearing just before you need to press them (soft blue/white, 1-2 seconds ahead, predictive)
- Note names floating above the keys in a clean sans-serif Apple-style font
- A subtle pulse to carry the timing
- A progress bar running along the edge of the session

Other scenario UI elements developed (painting and photography scenarios):
- A "Your Vision" mood board panel: three small curated reference photos in frosted glass cards
- Soft compositional nudges: gentle white text like "shift left slightly" that fades in and out
- A light reading indicator with a small directional diagram
- A reference photo card sliding in occasionally from a relevant artist, labeled with name and year
- A color suggestion panel with paint swatches (painting scenario)

Overall UI aesthetic: calm, clean, speculative. AVP-style spatial UI, frosted glass, persistent (never disappears), layered over raw, unaltered, real environments. The environment stays real. The UI is the star.

## 3. The Film: The Actual Deliverable

### What it is
A fake product launch film that looks and feels like it genuinely came from a future Aotearoa. Not a documentary about the product. Not an explainer of how it works. Not a tech demo. A launch film living inside the world of that future.

### The reference point that unlocked it (Week 9 crit)
**The Apple Watch ad.** Cinematic, minimal, emotional. Something that makes you feel what it would be like to use the device rather than understand how it works. What makes those Apple ads work is not the technology they show, it is the emotional truth they sell. When Apple launched the Apple Watch, the campaign was about identity, about the kind of person who wears this and why it matters to their life, not features. That is the approach for Compass. The device is almost beside the point. The film needs to communicate the feeling of having something that works **with you** rather than **for you or instead of you**.

Another recurring reference throughout the semester: the Cluely launch videos (high production, provocative, product-forward speculative tone), and Clearly-style first-person POV AR interface concept films.

### Tone
Quiet and understated. A creator in a normal session with the device on, the collaboration playing out naturally, letting that speak for itself rather than over-explaining what you are watching.

### One draft structure already explored (40-second concept version, from experiments)
A first-person POV film cutting between three scenes of roughly 12 seconds each:
1. **Piano**: hesitant beginner at a Yamaha digital keyboard in a casual bedroom, Compass guiding notes.
2. **Painting**: a person at an easel painting a vase, color suggestions and a mood board floating in frosted glass.
3. **Photography**: a person framing a shot indoors, soft compositional nudge appearing near center.

This is a concept draft, not the final script. The final film's length, script, and structure are open for DES304.

### Why the film matters (positioning)
So much of the conversation around AI, especially in San Francisco startup culture where Polo spent time, is about full replacement: every job, every creative act eventually handed to a machine. The doomers believe it is inevitable. Compass represents a different version of that future: one where technology amplifies rather than absorbs, where the creative practitioner in Aotearoa (the filmmaker, the designer, the musician) still sits at the centre of their own work with something genuinely useful alongside them. The project answers the brief question of how emerging technologies might transform making practices and understandings of creativity in Aotearoa: **not by replacing the maker, but by changing what it feels like to make.**

## 4. Theoretical Anchors

- **Dunne & Raby (2013), Speculative Everything**: speculative design does not predict or sell a future, it opens a space where the audience can ask whether they want it. The launch film format does exactly this: presenting something real enough to be desirable while leaving genuinely open the question of whether you should want it.
- **Kirby (2010), diegetic prototypes**: an artifact that looks and feels like it genuinely came from a future world. The launch film is a diegetic prototype. This concept came directly from the Emerging Technologies brief and sealed Polo's stream choice.
- **Schon (1983)**: reflection-in-action vs reflection-on-action, used to frame the making/reflecting rhythm of the process.
- Citations in APA 7th edition.

### The How Might We question (refined with Claude, after Jed flagged the brief-level HMW was too broad)
**"How might we use speculative design to help everyday creators in Aotearoa feel what it means to work alongside AI, rather than be replaced by it?"**

### The critical conceptual separation (from Jed, foundational)
Two things must never be confused:
1. **The AI technology depicted inside the work** (Compass, the fictional wearable in the film's world)
2. **The AI tools used to make the work** (Claude, Claude Code, Higgsfield, Kling, Gemini, etc.)

If the AR glasses were both the thing being discussed and the means of making the thing, the audience would not understand what the project is about. The emerging technology the project itself engages with (for brief purposes) includes AI video/image generation and AI coding tools. Jed also flagged that AI-generated material in the workflow needs careful citation and documentation.

## 5. Where the Film Is At: Solved and Unsolved

### Solved / settled
- Concept, product name (Compass), tone, and reference language are locked
- The Apple-Watch-ad framing is confirmed by crit feedback as the right lane
- UI design language is developed and articulated (the piano scenario is the clearest expression)
- Product design direction is mapped against real wearables (closest to Brilliant Labs Frame)
- The prompt intermediary workflow for AI generation is proven (see Section 6)
- The narrative positioning (assist not replace, Aotearoa creative at the centre) is defended through the reverse brief and crits

### The central unsolved problem: compositing
Getting the Compass headset display to look convincing on camera is the technical wall that could make or break the film's credibility. This has been the consistent unsolved problem since Week 7. What has been tried:

1. **Full AI generation** (Higgsfield, Kling, Gemini): produced footage but no real control over what it was saying visually. Outputs were not great. Not viable for the final film on its own.
2. **Real footage + AI compositing of UI overlays**: the UI elements did not sit convincingly on the footage. The compositing broke the believability it was supposed to create. Failed.
3. **Python + OpenCV compositing script**: built to process real piano footage with UI elements overlaid (glowing keys, note names, pulse, progress bar). Partially successful, but some UI elements dropped out in the output. Not clean enough for the final film.
4. **After Effects**: installed, tutorials started, orientation only. No execution yet. This is the most likely professional-grade path forward and a skill to build in DES304.

The known realistic paths (from research): manual compositing in After Effects or DaVinci Resolve Fusion (most polished), code-based MediaPipe hand tracking + OpenCV (most accurate), or Runway video-to-video (least predictable, alters footage).

### Other open questions for DES304
- Full world-building: what year exactly, what has changed in Aotearoa by then, why does Compass exist, who needs it
- Script and final film structure
- Whether to build a physical prop/mock-up of the glasses (was part of the original Week 4 vision; the crit's "design the product first" note points back at this)
- A planned "bad camera test" experiment: how much does production quality actually matter before committing to a direction (flagged in Week 8, not yet run)
- Music: Suno experiments failed (could not reference existing pieces, motion-graphic-style cinematic launch sounds did not land after hours of attempts). Sound direction remains open. ElevenLabs is available for voice work.
- Shooting logistics for footage that reads as a commercial rather than a demo: lighting, scenes, what the device looks like on camera at different distances (rough notes exist from Week 9)

### Footage experiments already shot
- Someone wearing the headset while painting
- Someone wearing the headset while taking photos
- Real piano footage for the OpenCV compositing test
These proved the device can feel real on camera and the footage can carry launch-film energy, but the UI compositing on top is where it broke.

## 6. The Production Workflow Discovery (Proven, Reusable)

**The prompt intermediary workflow.** Describing ideas naturally to generative tools produces confident-looking noise. The proven process:
1. Describe the idea to Claude in natural language
2. Claude writes a refined, detailed prompt (specific physical setup, persistent UI that never disappears, raw unaltered environment; that combination was the winning formula)
3. That prompt goes into the generation tool (Higgsfield, Kling, Gemini for video; image tools for storyboards)

This produces noticeably better output every time and became the storyboarding workflow for the whole project. Polo already uses it outside the course. It is a small shift in process and a significant shift in results.

Related insight: these tools reward people who already have a clear vision. If you know what you want, they get you there faster. If you do not, they produce noise. This observation is itself thematically connected to the project: it is about what happens to creatives who let the tool think for them instead of developing their own thinking first.

## 8. The Secondary Artifact: MUSE (The Interactive Game)

Confirmed as the close-second direction. A browser-based narrative game inspired by Emily is Away, built in Claude Code.

### Concept
Set inside a fake chat interface (ChatGPT-style) in Auckland 2035. The player is a creative working on a short film, talking to an AI creative companion called MUSE. At every decision point they choose whether to lean on the AI or trust themselves. Lean on it and things move faster but something gets lost. Resist and it is slower but it feels more like theirs. The game never tells them which was right. All responses are pre-written, not live-generated, so the emotional arc stays fully authored. The meta angle is deliberate: using an AI coding tool to build a game about the AI-human creative relationship.

### V2 build (current state)
- **Architecture**: four files (index.html, style.css, story.js, game.js). story.js separated so all dialogue and branching can be edited without touching code.
- **Structure**: three days with distinct scenes. Day 1: concept development and location scouting. Day 2: on set, a planned shot is not working and an unplanned one might be better. Day 3: the edit, the final call on the defining shot of the film. Strictly one-on-one, player and MUSE, no other characters.
- **Opening**: game starts automatically (no typed trigger). Opening message: "I'm working on a short film and I'm kind of stuck. Can you help me develop the concept?" (written to mirror how someone actually opens ChatGPT)
- **Choices**: two clickable buttons with full keyboard support (press 1 or 2)
- **Timing** (tuned): 2000ms typing indicator before MUSE messages, 800ms between consecutive messages, 1200ms pause before choices, 2500ms auto-next delay
- **leanScore system**: 0 to 5, tracking how much creative control the player cedes
- **Five endings**, each a fundamentally different relationship to authorship:
  1. The Purist (score 0-1)
  2. The Collaborator
  3. The Negotiator
  4. The Passenger
  5. The Ghost (score 5)
- **UI corruption mechanic**: CSS custom properties update after every choice. Low score: clean, warm, minimal. High score: cold blue-black background, purple-tinted borders on MUSE's bubbles, scanlines, a vignette creeping in, a MUSE influence bar filling in the header. The interface itself reflects how much of the creative work has shifted to MUSE. The player feels it without being told.
- Has a title screen.

### Role going forward
Secondary artifact and continued development target. It puts an audience inside the same question the film explores, in interactive form. The film shows the optimistic answer; the game lets you live the tension.

## 9. What Was Dropped and Why

**The Aotearoa Creative** (speculative journalism publication, a fake ~2030 media outlet with articles, radio segments, a downloadable newspaper, and a pessimistic editorial tone reporting on AI's aftermath for creative workers). A prototype was built in Claude Code (masthead, at least one article, radio segment placeholder). It did not survive the Week 9 crit. The killing questions: where does this go, what skill are you building, do you actually care about this long term? Polo had no answer to the passion question. Journalism is conceptually interesting to him but he has no investment in it as a practice. This echoed Jed's earlier advice that the work should connect to something Polo genuinely wants to get better at. Dropped cleanly, no regrets.

## 10. Positionality (Must Be Woven Into All Future Reflective Work)

Polo runs The Next Something, a cinematic media studio for startup founders and venture-backed companies (previously also NDLEMVRS, stepped back from). He makes films professionally: shooting, editing, pitching, managing editors, international clients. This is why the launch film direction is a deepening, not a stretch.

The tensions to keep naming honestly:
- He spends his time in startup and media spaces where AI is exciting and people are builders and optimists. That is not the average New Zealand creative's relationship with the technology. For many, the threat feels real and immediate, not speculative.
- His perspective is shaped heavily by San Francisco (the trip, the doomers, the AI-saturated culture). The brief asks specifically about Aotearoa and New Zealand stakeholders. Those are not the same context.
- The standing defence: the work is not trying to represent Polo's perspective on AI. It creates an experience and leaves the question open.
- Final presentation peer feedback confirmed this needs active bridging: the startup/international background is useful but pulls away from the Aotearoa specificity of the brief unless consciously tied back. Name it, then bridge it, do not leave it implicit.

## 12. Hard Rules for Any Work On This Project

- **Never use em dashes in any writing. Ever.**
- Blog voice: casual, first person, short punchy sentences mixed with longer explanatory ones, minimal subheadings, grounded in specific actions not abstractions, genuine uncertainty rather than overclaiming.
- Positionality woven in naturally, never a standalone section.
- All documented work must be authentic: real experiments only, defensible in a final presentation.
- Keep the depicted technology (Compass) and the making tools (Claude etc.) conceptually separate at all times.
- Polo prefers Claude to generate from verbal prompts and prior context, then iterate together step by step. He pushes back directly when outputs miss. Search existing context before asking him to re-explain.
- IRC framework (Experience, Reflection on Action, Theory, Preparation) is the reflective structure from Week 4 onward.
